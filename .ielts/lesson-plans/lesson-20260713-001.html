☕ Tea: From Ancient China to the World

According to Chinese legend, tea was discovered accidentally by Emperor Shen Nung in 2737 BCE when leaves from a nearby tea bush fell into a pot of water he was boiling. However, the earliest reliable historical records of tea consumption date from the Tang Dynasty (618-907 CE), when tea became a popular beverage throughout China. During this period, Cha Jing (The Classic of Tea), the world's first book about tea, was written by Lu Yu, a scholar who spent years studying tea cultivation and preparation.

Tea spread beyond China through Buddhist monks who traveled between China, Korea, and Japan. The Japanese tea ceremony, chanoyu, developed in the 16th century under the influence of Zen Buddhism, transforming tea drinking from a simple refreshment into a spiritual practice. Unlike the Chinese, who preferred loose-leaf tea, the Japanese developed a powdered form called matcha, which remains central to the tea ceremony today.

Tea was introduced to Europe in the early 17th century by Portuguese and Dutch traders. It became enormously popular in Britain during the 18th century, partly due to the efforts of Catherine of Braganza, the Portuguese wife of King Charles II, who made tea-drinking fashionable at court. The British later established large tea plantations in India during the 19th century to compete with China's monopoly on tea production. Today, tea is the most widely consumed beverage in the world after water, with China remaining the largest producer.
